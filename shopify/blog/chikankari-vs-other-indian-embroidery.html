India has dozens of embroidery traditions, and many of them look similar at first glance in an online photo. Is that white-on-white floral work Chikankari, or something else? Is that mirror work from Kutch? And is any of it actually stitched by hand? Here is a simple guide to how Chikankari compares with India's other famous embroideries, and how to spot real hand-work in any of them.

Chikankari (Lucknow, Uttar Pradesh)

What it looks like: delicate floral vines, paisleys and buttis, traditionally white thread on white or pastel fabric, though today you will find it in every colour.
Key stitches: bakhiya (shadow work, stitched from the back so the thread glows softly through the fabric), tepchi (running stitch), phanda and murri (tiny knots) and jaali (net-like openwork made by pulling threads apart).
Fabric: light, breathable fabrics like cotton, mul, georgette and chiffon.
How to recognise it: fine, soft, airy embroidery that sits almost inside the cloth, with shadow-work visible from the front. It is subtle rather than bold.

For a detailed checklist on spotting machine copies, read Real vs Machine Chikankari: How to Spot Hand Embroidery.

Kutchi embroidery and mirror work (Kutch, Gujarat)

What it looks like: bold, bright and dense, with small round mirrors (abhla) held in place by colourful thread, geometric motifs, peacocks and flowers. Different communities of Kutch, such as the Rabari, Ahir and Mutva, each have their own style.
Key stitches: chain stitch, interlacing, buttonhole stitch around mirrors and satin stitch.
How to recognise it: strong colour, a lot of texture and mirrors that catch the light. Where Chikankari whispers, Kutchi work celebrates.

Read more in Colours of Kutch: Ajrakh, Patchwork and Mirror Work.

Phulkari (Punjab)

What it looks like: "flower work" in glossy silk floss, often in orange, gold, pink and red, with geometric flowers covering large areas of the fabric. In a bagh ("garden") the embroidery covers the whole cloth.
Key stitch: a long darning stitch worked from the back of the fabric.
How to recognise it: shiny, flat blocks of colour in diagonal and geometric patterns, very different from Chikankari's fine floral lines.

Kantha (West Bengal and Odisha)

What it looks like: rows of small running stitches that ripple across the fabric, sometimes forming pictures of animals, people and flowers. Kantha was traditionally used to layer old saris into quilts.
Key stitch: a simple running stitch, repeated thousands of times.
How to recognise it: a slightly wrinkled, textured surface from all the running stitches. Chikankari's tepchi is also a running stitch, but Kantha covers far more of the fabric.

Kashidakari (Kashmir)

What it looks like: flowing chinar leaves, vines, birds and paisleys in rich colours, often on wool shawls and pherans.
Key stitches: chain stitch, stem stitch and satin stitch, sometimes worked with a hooked needle (aari).
How to recognise it: colourful, nature-inspired designs on warmer fabrics, more filled-in than Chikankari.

Zardozi (Lucknow, Agra and other centres)

What it looks like: heavy, glittering metallic embroidery in gold and silver thread, often with beads, sequins and stones, worked on silk and velvet for bridal and festive wear.
How to recognise it: raised, metallic and weighty. Lucknow is famous for both Chikankari and Zardozi, and some festive Chikankari pieces combine the two.

Mukaish (Lucknow)

Mukaish, also called badla, is a Lucknowi technique where thin strips of metal wire are twisted into the fabric to make tiny shining dots. It is often added to Chikankari for festive sparkle, so a "Chikankari with mukaish" kurta combines both crafts.

How to spot real hand-work in any embroidery

Whatever the craft, the same simple checks help you tell hand embroidery from machine copies:

  1. Turn it over. Hand embroidery shows knots, thread ends and slight differences on the back. Machine embroidery usually has a very regular back, often with a backing sheet or extra bobbin thread running across the design.
  2. Compare two motifs. Hand-stitched flowers are never exact copies of each other. If every motif is identical down to the last stitch, a machine probably made it.
  3. Look at the stitch length. Hand stitches vary slightly in length and tension. Machine stitches are perfectly even.
  4. Check the mirrors. In real mirror work, each mirror is held by a ring of hand stitches. Glued or printed "mirrors" are a sign of a shortcut.
  5. Feel the fabric. Hand embroidery moves with the cloth. Dense machine embroidery on synthetic fabric often feels stiff.
  6. Be realistic about price. Hand embroidery takes hours or even days. If a "hand-embroidered" piece costs less than a plain printed one, be careful.

Small irregularities are not flaws. They are proof that an artisan made your piece by hand.
