Deep indigo, rich madder red, black outlines and rows of star-like geometric patterns: once you have seen Ajrakh, you recognise it anywhere. It is one of the oldest block-printing traditions in the world, and it is still made by hand in Kutch, Gujarat. Here is what Ajrakh is, where it comes from and how a single piece of cloth is made.

What is Ajrakh?

Ajrakh is a hand-block printing and resist-dyeing craft from the desert regions of Kutch in Gujarat, Barmer in Rajasthan and Sindh across the border. The cloth is printed with carved wooden blocks, painted with mud and lime pastes that resist dye, and dyed again and again in indigo and red. The result is a layered, slightly soft-edged pattern with a depth that flat printing cannot copy.

Traditional Ajrakh uses a small, meaningful palette:

  • Indigo blue for the sky and night
  • Madder or alizarin red for the earth
  • Black, made from iron, for outlines and definition
  • White, where the resist paste has protected the cloth

Where does the name come from?

Nobody is completely sure. A popular story says it comes from aaj rakh, "keep it today", because each stage of dyeing needs the cloth to rest overnight. Another view links it to azrak, the Arabic word for blue, a nod to Ajrakh's signature indigo.

A short history

Ajrakh belongs to a block-printing tradition in north-west India and Sindh that goes back many centuries. The statue of the "priest-king" from Mohenjo-daro wears a shawl with trefoil patterns that are often compared to Ajrakh motifs, and fragments of block-printed Gujarati cotton have been found as far away as Fustat in Egypt, showing how widely these textiles travelled along old trade routes.

In Kutch, the craft has been carried for generations by the Khatri community of printers and dyers. Ajrakh was traditionally worn by local herding and farming communities, especially the Maldhari, as turbans, lungis and shoulder cloths, and each community had its own preferred patterns.

For a long time the centre of Ajrakh in Kutch was the village of Dhamadka. After the 2001 Gujarat earthquake changed the quality of the river water the dyers relied on, many families moved and founded a new village, Ajrakhpur, near Bhuj. Today Ajrakhpur is one of the best-known places in the world for the craft.

How Ajrakh is made: the process

A traditional Ajrakh cloth goes through more than a dozen stages and can take two to three weeks, because the fabric has to be washed, dried in the sun and rested between steps. The exact recipe varies from workshop to workshop, but the main stages are:

  1. Saaj (preparing the cloth): the cotton is washed and soaked in a mix that traditionally includes soda ash, castor oil and camel dung, then left to soften. This helps the cloth take up dye evenly.
  2. Harda (mordanting): the cloth is dipped in a solution of harda (myrobalan nut). It turns a pale yellow and is now ready to bind with the dyes that follow.
  3. Rekh (outlines): the first blocks print the outline of the design with a resist paste of lime and gum, which will keep these lines white.
  4. Kat (black): blocks print a black made from scrap iron fermented with jaggery, which reacts with the harda to give a strong, permanent black.
  5. Gach (mud resist): a paste of clay, alum and other ingredients is printed over the areas that should turn red. Sawdust or cow dung is sprinkled on top to help it dry.
  6. Indigo dyeing: the cloth is dipped in indigo vats. Everything not protected by resist turns blue.
  7. Washing and alum printing: the cloth is washed, then printed again where the red should appear.
  8. Red dyeing: the cloth is boiled with madder or alizarin. The red binds only where alum was printed, and the other colours stay put.
  9. Final washing and sun-drying: repeated washing brings out the clear whites and the full depth of the colours.

Traditionally, the best Ajrakh was printed on both sides of the cloth with perfect alignment, a skill that takes years to learn.

Ajrakh motifs

Ajrakh patterns are mostly geometric and symmetrical, built on a grid. You will often see stars, rosettes, trefoils, jaali (lattice) designs and a central repeating medallion surrounded by borders. Because many Ajrakh printers come from Muslim families, the designs traditionally avoid human and animal figures.

How to recognise real hand-block Ajrakh

Ajrakh's popularity means there are many screen-printed and digitally printed "Ajrakh-style" fabrics. These are not bad clothes, but they are not the hand craft. A few simple checks:

  • Look at the reverse. On hand-dyed Ajrakh the colour soaks deep into the fabric, so the back is almost as strong as the front. Surface prints look pale or white on the back.
  • Look at the edges. Resist-dyeing gives slightly soft, organic edges and small breaks in the lines where blocks meet. Perfectly sharp, identical repeats usually mean machine printing.
  • Look at the colours. Traditional Ajrakh has deep, slightly earthy tones rather than very bright, flat colour.
  • Smell it. New naturally dyed Ajrakh can have a faint earthy smell that fades after a few washes.

Many modern workshops also make Ajrakh-inspired hand-block prints with fewer stages or different dyes. That is still hand-block printing, just a lighter take on the full traditional process, and it is usually easier on the budget.

Wearing and caring for Ajrakh

Ajrakh's bold patterns work best with simple accessories: oxidised silver jewellery, juttis or kolhapuris and a plain dupatta or none at all. Cotton Ajrakh is perfect for everyday wear and travel; silk Ajrakh is a beautiful choice for festivals and cultural events.
